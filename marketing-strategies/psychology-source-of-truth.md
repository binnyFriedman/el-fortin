## 1. Principles (what the studies found)

| # | Finding | Source |
|---|---|---|
| P1 | Experienced private investors blend analysis + intuition; when they conflict, **gut feel about the entrepreneur wins** — and predicts returns 4 years out. | Huang & Pearce, *Managing the Unknowable*, Administrative Science Quarterly (2015) |
| P2 | People who must **justify decisions to third parties rely on analysis**, not gut. | Same research program (LSE summary) |
| P3 | Investors avoid unfamiliar/foreign assets (**home bias**), driven by **ambiguity aversion** — comfort, not information. | Huberman, *Familiarity Breeds Investment*, RFS (2001); German local-bias studies |
| P4 | Hard-to-pronounce names are judged **riskier**; processing fluency drives perceived safety. | Song & Schwarz (2009); Alter & Oppenheimer (2006) |
| P5 | Borrowers whose **faces** were rated more trustworthy got funded more often and ~50bp cheaper; the perception was accurate. | Duarte, Siegel & Young, *Trust and Credit*, RFS (2012) |
| P6 | A **founding story** significantly increases funding probability — *only if* congruent with the venture. Passion alone doesn't move investors; coherent identity does. | Martens, Jennings & Jennings, AMJ (2007); Shark Tank corpus + experiment (JRME 2025) |
| P7 | Two-sided messages (admitting a negative) raise credibility; a small blemish **after** positives increases persuasion. | Crowley & Hoyer (1994); Ein-Gar, Shiv & Tormala, JCR (2012) |
| P8 | **Pratfall boundary:** admitting flaws increases liking **only for already-competent sources**; for unknowns it lowers evaluation. | Aronson, Willerman & Floyd (1966) |
| P9 | Detected persuasion attempts get discounted (**Persuasion Knowledge Model**). Unsolicited + foreign + high-yield = the scam pattern. | Friestad & Wright (1994) |
| P10 | Investors strongly prefer **co-ethnic / in-group** founders; shared identity ("Unity") is the strongest influence lever. | Bengtsson & Hsu; Hegde & Tumlinson, Mgmt Science (2014); Cialdini, *Pre-Suasion* (2016) |
| P11 | **Caution:** affinity-*only* investing underperforms, and sophisticated investors know it. | Gompers, Mukharlyamov & Xuan, *The Cost of Friendship*, NBER (2012) |
| P12 | **Betrayal aversion:** losses caused by a trusted person hurt far more than chance losses. One collapsed claim reclassifies the seller from person to predator. | Bohnet & Zeckhauser (2004) |
| P13 | Trust — not information — drives participation in financial markets; trust in a person can substitute for familiarity with an asset. | Guiso, Sapienza & Zingales, *Trusting the Stock Market*, JF (2008) |
| P14 | Loss framing beats gain framing for loss-averse audiences (**prospect theory**); an **identifiable person** triggers care where statistics don't. | Kahneman & Tversky (1979); Small & Loewenstein (2003) |
| P15 | Explicitly affirming the right to refuse (**"but you are free"**) roughly doubles compliance across 42 studies. | Carpenter meta-analysis (2013) |
| P16 | Scarcity persuades **only when credible**. | Cialdini, *Influence*; Worchel et al. (1975) |
| P17 | **Large type signals importance** and guides selective memory. Enlarging numerical claims also increases their perceived message strength and persuasiveness. Typography therefore determines what the page appears to be selling, not merely what readers notice. | Luna, Nogueira & Albuquerque, *Memory* (2019); Murphy et al., *Memory & Cognition* (2024); Park & Kang, *Journal of Consumer Behaviour* (2025) |

## 2. Audience physics (derived from P1–P3)

Two psychologically distinct buyer types exist for any private deal:

| | End investor (decides for self) | Intermediary (answers to a client) |
|---|---|---|
| Decision mode | Gut feel about the person (P1) | Justification / blame-avoidance (P2) |
| Dominant fear | Ambiguity — foreign market, unknown seller (P3) | Being blamed for a bad recommendation |
| What converts | Trust in an identifiable, competent, candid human (P5, P8, P13) | Defensible documents and conservative numbers |
| Role of yield | Confirms a decision already forming | The *stress/floor* number is the usable one |

Any funnel must treat these as separate tracks; a single asset cannot optimally serve both.

## 4. Sequencing law (derived; applies to any asset)

The psychologically valid order of elements in any cold-facing asset:

1. **Familiar anchor** — fluent, known reference points (P3, P4)
2. **Competence** — verifiable track record (P8)
3. **Person + story** — identifiable human, congruent biography (P1, P5, P6, P14)
4. **Candor block** — what it is not; only after 1–3 (P7, P8)
5. **The number** — floor/stress first; targets labeled as targets (P12)
6. **Credible scarcity** — real counts only (P16)
7. **Low-commitment CTA + explicit freedom to refuse** (P15)

## 5. Red lines (violations predicted to backfire)

- No yield figures in cold subject lines or opening seconds (P9).
- No absolute claims — "guaranteed", "completely insulated", "risk-free" (P12).
- No manufactured urgency; only real inventory counts (P16).
- No candor block before a competence signal (P8).
- No high-commitment first ask; commitment must ramp (foot-in-the-door gradient).
- No affinity pitch that bypasses documents (P11).
- No unverifiable biographical or market claims (P12; pitch doc §8 ledger governs market claims).
- No inconsistent persona across assets — one voice everywhere (consistency principle).
- No oversized yield, floor, price, or scarcity claims. Large display type is reserved for the ownership frame, verifiable competence, and low-pressure next step; financial figures remain clearly legible but evidentiary (P9, P12, P17).
